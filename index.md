## Assistant Professor and Faculty Fellow at the [NYU Center for Data Science](https://cds.nyu.edu/)

<img class="profile-picture" src="sherlock.jpg">

Mom, you remember I'm a political scientist, right? Well, you know what, things have been changing really fast in our field these last few years because of generative AI and all these computational methods. I'm very optimistic about all the mess that's going on around us! We can now revisit old theories with a much stronger microscope and pick up subtle patterns in political behavior that older approaches couldn't see. I'm taking advantage of this brave new world to study how political identity organizes the way people make decisions and, the other way around, how you can take a person's behavior and work backward to recover what their political identity actually is. My broader agenda is to "embed" political identity inside computer vision tools themselves, so that identity stops being something bolted on after the fact and becomes part of how the model **``sees``** behavior from the start. I believe this matters a great deal for computational political science. I'm on the job market this year, wish me luck!

I see my research as situated primarily within the following fields:

- [x] Computational Political Behavior (**``CPB``**)
- [x] Political Communication (**``PC``**)

- Visual Political Polarization. (PAP) (**``AP``**) (**``PC``**) (**``CPB``**)
 <details>
  <summary style="margin-left: 70px;"><font size="-1"><b>Abstract</b></font></summary>
  <p style="margin-left: 70px;"><font size="-1">
Theories of political polarization assume that people who see the same content share a common basis for judgment, yet information sampling, the stage at which people select which information to take in, remains the least studied stage of polarization. I develop a theory of visual political polarization and use webcam eye tracking to provide the first quantification of how much the sampling channel contributes to polarization. Pilot evidence suggests that issue positions shape how opponents examine and evaluate visual information. Experimentally activating partisan identity moves gaze yet leaves disagreement at baseline. However, disagreement narrows when an exogenous change in how people view political material changes the information they extract from it, because their evaluations follow that information. The sampling channel therefore offers a previously overlooked opportunity to reduce polarization at the stage of information acquisition, before people form judgments. More importantly, differences in information sampling can recur with every exposure in the wild and compound into far wider disagreement in evaluations than any single viewing produces. </font></p>
